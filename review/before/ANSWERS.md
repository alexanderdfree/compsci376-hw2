I picked it because it is sentence-segmented (`.sents()` works, which the language-model
code requires), its formal register makes it easy to judge whether a generated sentence
"sounds like a sentence", and it is small enough that every experiment runs in about two
seconds. One wrinkle: the post-2009 addresses are stored as UTF-8 but NLTK decodes them as
Latin-1, so curly quotes and em-dashes become junk tokens (`\x80\x94`, `¡`) and an `â`
gets glued onto the preceding word. That shows up in question 3.

## 3. Did punctuation tokens impact sentence generation? How?

Yes, in four distinct ways.

**Bad starting points.** The random-start script picks a starting bigram uniformly from all
67,961 distinct contexts, and 8,841 of them (13%) contain punctuation, so sentences begin
mid-clause or even on a comma. From `output/basic_lm_random_start.txt` and
`output/improved_frequent_start.txt`:

> of magnanimity , one home , take it .
> relationship .
> exposition belongs .
> , as to retain persons known to all .
> , invading neither the narrow confines of a similar character , situation , is needed to secure …

"relationship ." and "exposition belongs ." happen when the chosen bigram is
`(word, ".")`: the only thing the model has ever seen after a period is the end-of-sentence
padding, so the sentence stops immediately. Over 500 generated sentences, only 9% (uniform
start) and 6% (frequency-weighted start) began with a capitalised word.

**Good terminators.** Because `.` is an ordinary token that is always followed by the `None`
padding, the model has effectively learned "period means stop": 99-100% of generated
sentences in every punctuation-kept configuration end with terminal punctuation. Stripping
punctuation (`--punct strip`) removes that stopping cue and every comma, and the output turns
into run-ons with no internal structure (mean 26.2 words, 0/500 ending with terminal
punctuation, and one 124-word run-on in `output/improved_boundary_nopunct.txt`).

**Comma chains.** Kept commas are the glue that lets a trigram model keep going with only
two words of memory: the longest sentence in `output/basic_lm.txt` is 84 words, and the
longer a sentence gets the further it drifts from whatever it started saying.

**Junk tokens.** The mojibake survives the punctuation filter and gets generated:

> … and we will seize itâ  so long as the woe due to the present have turned their thoughts toward home …

(`output/basic_lm.txt`, the `â` is the first byte of a mangled em-dash). `improved_lm.py`
strips non-ASCII bytes from every token before training, which removes these.

## 4. Were you able to generate reasonable sentences? What did you try?

Running the instructor's random-start code unchanged on `inaugural` mostly produced
fragments (see question 3). I then rewrote it as `improved_lm.py`, keeping the same
count-then-sample model but exposing each tweak as a flag, and measured every configuration
on 500 sentences (`output/comparison.txt`):

| configuration | mean words | verbatim corpus copies | starts with capital | ends with . ! ? |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| original: uniform random starting bigram | 25.9 | 0% | 9% | 99% |
| start from a frequent bigram (frequency-weighted) | 26.2 | 0% | 6% | 99% |
| start from the sentence boundary | 27.4 | 2% | 99% | 99% |
| boundary start, punctuation stripped | 26.2 | 2% | 100% | 0% |
| boundary start, bigram model (1 word of context) | 28.5 | 0% | 100% | 100% |
| boundary start, 4-gram model (3 words of context) | 26.1 | 18% | 100% | 99% |
| **boundary start, trigram, 6-30 words, reject verbatim** | **15.9** | **0%** | **99%** | **100%** |

What each tweak did:

1. **Start at the sentence boundary** (`--start boundary`). The model already contains the
   context `(None, None)`, whose continuations are exactly the words that open real
   sentences (The, We, It, I, …). Sampling the first two words from there instead of picking
   a random bigram is the direct fix for the hint in the assignment: `of the` is the most
   frequent bigram in the corpus, but not one of the 5,395 sentences begins with lowercase `of the` (four begin with
   "Of the"), so it is a terrible starting point. The most frequent *sentence-initial* bigrams are `It is`,
   `We have`, `We will`, and boundary sampling picks them in proportion. Capitalised starts
   went from 9% to 99%. Weighting the random start by bigram frequency (`--start frequent`)
   made things worse (6%), which confirms that frequency and "good starting point" are
   different properties.
2. **Cleaner sampling.** The accumulate-until-`r` loop became `random.choices` weighted by
   raw counts (same distribution, no normalisation pass, no floating-point edge case), with a
   hard length cap so a sentence can never loop forever.
3. **N-gram order** (`--context K`). I kept the assignment's bigram context (a trigram
   model) as the default and compared it with one and three words of context. The bigram
   model never copies the corpus but wanders, because it forgets everything but the last word:
   > The snow was to foster capital , the independence , rather might such high place to act , and record numbers .

   The 4-gram model reads beautifully but 18% of its output (8 of the 20 in
   `output/improved_4gram_model.txt`) is copied verbatim, for example Nixon's 1969
   "We see tasks that need doing , waiting for hands to do them ." With 124,020 distinct
   three-word contexts in a 156k-word corpus, most contexts have only one possible
   continuation, so the model has memorised the speeches. The trigram model is the sweet
   spot at 2% copies.
4. **Length filter and novelty check** (`--min-len 6 --max-len 30 --novel`). A trigram model
   drifts in proportion to length, so rejecting anything under 6 or over 30 words (about 40%
   of draws) and anything that appears verbatim in the corpus roughly halves the mean length
   and makes most survivors coherent.
5. **Punctuation stripping** was tried and rejected (question 3): keeping punctuation and
   simply never *starting* on it, which boundary starts guarantee, works better.

Final configuration:

```bash
python improved_lm.py --start boundary --context 2 --min-len 6 --max-len 30 --novel --seed 376
```

Sample output (`output/improved_final.txt`, none of these sentences occur in the corpus):

> It is time rather to speak .
> These basic precepts are not at fault .
> I will faithfully observe the rights and equal citizenship .
> This Administration has come to us all .
> God bless you and God bless America .
> Universal suffrage should rest upon the whole Government and those reserved to the proper occasion .
> Passion has swept some of its whole constitutional vigor , and uncompromising opponent of every free citizen everywhere to place the cause of peace .

"God bless you and God bless America ." is a nice illustration: the exact sentence never
appears in any address, but the model stitched two formulaic endings together into something
a president could plausibly say. The failures are just as instructive:

> Look , folks , all the markets of the advisers of my countrymen are just and advantageous to both purposes .
> We need all our earthly hopes are entwined .

The first welds Biden's 2021 "Look, folks" onto nineteenth-century diction, and the second is
fine at every three-word window but ungrammatical as a whole. Two words of context carry no
notion of topic, era, register, or long-range agreement, which is the fundamental ceiling of
a count-based n-gram model, and the reason the next step up is smoothing and interpolation
across orders, a much larger corpus (`--corpus state_union` or `gutenberg` work out of the
box), or a neural language model.

So: yes, with boundary starts, a trigram model, and a length/novelty filter, most generated
sentences are reasonable short inaugural-sounding sentences; long ones still drift.
