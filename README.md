## 1. Which corpus did you choose?

(1789) through Trump's second inauguration (2025).

| genesis | 315,268 | 13,644 | 8 |
| treebank | 100,676 | 3,914 | 199 |

I picked it because it supports sentence tokenization through `.sents()`, which the
language-model code requires, its formal register makes generated sentences easy to compare,
and it is small enough to run the experiments locally. The `punkt_tab` tokenizer data is
needed in addition to the corpus; [corpus_config.py](src/corpus_config.py) downloads both resources.

One wrinkle: the post-2009 addresses are stored as UTF-8 but this NLTK corpus reader decodes
them as Latin-1, so curly quotes and em-dashes become junk tokens such as `\x80\x94`, and
an `â` gets glued onto the preceding word. Older text also contains encoding artifacts
such as `¡`. That shows up in question 3.

With punctuation kept, the comma is the second most common token, and **five** of the top
ten bigrams and five of the top ten trigrams contain punctuation. The frequency scripts
operate on a flat token stream, so n-grams can cross sentence boundaries: the top trigram

Filtering punctuation creates additional adjacencies: `to the` rises from 716 to 718
because two occurrences of `to " the` lose their quotation mark. `. The` and `. We`
disappear entirely. Membership in `string.punctuation` is a **substring test**, not a
general test for whether every character is punctuation. Tokens such as `--` (363),
`."` (74), `,"` (23), `...` (5), and the mojibake tokens (`\x80\x94` 46, `¡` 18)
survive it. The filter removed 14,329 tokens (9.2%); all removed tokens in this corpus
happened to be single characters.

## 3. Did punctuation tokens impact sentence generation? How?

Yes, in four distinct ways.

**Bad starting points.** The random-start script picks a starting bigram uniformly from all
68,068 distinct contexts, and 8,187 of them (12.0%) contain a token with no letters or
digits, excluding the `None` padding. Sentences can therefore begin mid-clause or even on
a comma. From [output/basic_lm_random_start.txt](output/basic_lm_random_start.txt) and
[output/improved_frequent_start.txt](output/improved_frequent_start.txt):

> of magnanimity , one home , take it .
> relationship .
> exposition belongs .
> , as to retain persons known to all .
> , invading neither the narrow confines of a similar character , situation , is needed to secure …

"relationship ." starts from `('relationship', '.')`, which is followed by end padding.
"exposition belongs ." instead starts from `('exposition', 'belongs')` and generates the
period. In the rewritten generator's 500-sentence experiments, 8.6% of uniform-start outputs
and 5.8% of frequency-weighted-start outputs begin with a capitalised token. These rates
describe the cleaned model used in question 4, not the untouched baseline.

**Sentence endings.** A period is usually followed by `None` padding, but there are
exceptions: 37 of the corpus's 5,186 period tokens occur inside sentences, including
`Mr . Jefferson` and `St . Croix`. Generation ultimately stops on the `None` marker.
Across the punctuation-kept configurations in question 4, 98.8–100.0% of outputs end
with terminal punctuation.

Stripping punctuation (`--punct strip`) removes visible sentence and clause markers,
but the model still learns and samples `None` at sentence ends. The resulting text is
harder to read without those written cues: the mean is 26.2 tokens, 0/500 outputs end
with terminal punctuation, and one unpunctuated output has 124 tokens
([output/improved_boundary_nopunct.txt](output/improved_boundary_nopunct.txt)).

**Comma chains.** The generated text can chain clauses and drift from its opening topic.
The longest sentence in [output/basic_lm.txt](output/basic_lm.txt) is 84 tokens, including punctuation. Two
tokens of context are not enough to maintain a coherent argument through a long output.

**Junk tokens.** The mojibake survives the punctuation filter and gets generated:

> … and we will seize itâ  so long as the woe due to the present have turned their thoughts toward home …

([output/basic_lm.txt](output/basic_lm.txt), the `â` comes from misdecoding the first byte of an em-dash).
[improved_lm.py](src/improved_lm.py) strips non-ASCII characters from every token before training. This removes
the displayed artifacts, but is a lossy cleanup that can also remove legitimate characters.

## 4. Were you able to generate reasonable sentences? What did you try?

The adaptation of the instructor's random-start generator produced many fragments
(see question 3). I then rewrote it as [improved_lm.py](src/improved_lm.py), keeping the count-then-sample
approach but exposing each tweak as a flag. The table compares configurations of this
**rewritten generator**, each run on 500 sentences ([output/comparison.txt](output/comparison.txt)). Its uniform
start uses the original start-selection strategy, but the rewrite also changes preprocessing,
sampling, stopping, and the hard cap, so this row is not the original baseline.

All rows strip non-ASCII characters. The cleaned trigram model with punctuation kept has
67,961 contexts, compared with 68,068 in the raw baseline. “Verbatim copies” means exact
matches to corpus sentences after the same cleaning and punctuation processing as the model.
Terminal punctuation includes tokens such as `."` and `...`, as well as `.`, `!`, and `?`.

| configuration | mean tokens | verbatim corpus copies | starts with capital | ends with terminal punctuation |
|---|---:|---:|---:|---:|
| rewritten trigram, uniform random starting bigram | 25.9 | 0/500 (0.0%) | 8.6% | 98.8% |
| start from a frequent bigram (frequency-weighted) | 26.2 | 0/500 (0.0%) | 5.8% | 99.0% |
| start from the sentence boundary | 27.4 | 11/500 (2.2%) | 99.4% | 98.8% |
| boundary start, punctuation stripped | 26.2 | 8/500 (1.6%) | 99.6% | 0.0% |
| boundary start, bigram model (1 token of context) | 28.5 | 2/500 (0.4%) | 99.8% | 99.6% |
| boundary start, 4-gram model (3 tokens of context) | 26.1 | 89/500 (17.8%) | 100.0% | 99.0% |
| **boundary start, trigram, 6-30 tokens, reject verbatim** | **15.9** | **0/500 (0.0%)** | **99.2%** | **99.6%** |
| boundary start, bigram, 6-30 tokens, reject verbatim | 16.3 | 0/500 (0.0%) | 99.8% | 99.4% |

What each tweak did:

1. **Start at the sentence boundary** (`--start boundary`). The model already contains the
   context `(None, None)`, whose continuations are the tokens that open real
   sentences (The, We, It, I, quotation marks, parentheses, …). Sampling the first two tokens
   from there instead of picking
   a random bigram is the direct fix for the hint in the assignment: `of the` is the most
   frequent bigram in the corpus, but not one of the 5,395 sentences begins with lowercase `of the` (four begin with
   "Of the"), so it is a terrible starting point. The most frequent *sentence-initial* bigrams are `It is`,
   `We have`, `We will`, and boundary sampling picks them in proportion. In the rewritten
   trigram model, capitalised starts went from 8.6% with uniform starts to 99.4% with
   boundary starts. Weighting random starts by bigram frequency (`--start frequent`)
   lowered this measure to 5.8%, illustrating that frequency and sentence-initial suitability
   are different properties. Capitalisation alone does not measure sentence quality.
2. **Cleaner sampling.** The accumulate-until-`r` loop became `random.choices` weighted by
   raw counts, representing the same empirical transition probabilities for a fixed
   preprocessed corpus. A hard cap bounds the generation loop. This does not reproduce
   the baseline's exact seeded sequence, which also consumes different boundary draws.
3. **N-gram order** (`--context K`). I kept the assignment's bigram context (a trigram
   model) as the default and compared it with one and three tokens of context. The bigram
   model copied 2/500 corpus sentences (0.4%): `Thank you .` and `Amen .`. It also wanders,
   because it remembers only the previous token:
   > The snow was to foster capital , the independence , rather might such high place to act , and record numbers .

   The 4-gram model produces some fluent examples but copies 89/500 sentences (17.8%).
   In the smaller 20-sentence sample ([output/improved_4gram_model.txt](output/improved_4gram_model.txt)), 8/20 are copies,
   for example Nixon's 1969
   "We see tasks that need doing , waiting for hands to do them ." With 124,020 distinct
   three-token contexts, 113,863 (91.8%) have only one possible continuation, which helps
   explain why generation often follows memorised passages. I kept the trigram model as a
   compromise; its unfiltered boundary-start run copied 11/500 sentences (2.2%).
4. **Length filter and novelty check** (`--min-len 6 --max-len 30 --novel`). The filter
   accepts 6–30 tokens, including punctuation, and rejects exact cleaned-corpus sentence
   matches. Producing 500 accepted sentences took 867 draws: 358 were rejected for length
   and nine for verbatim copying. The mean dropped from 27.4 to 15.9 tokens, but neither
   the length filter nor the novelty check guarantees grammaticality or coherence.
5. **Punctuation stripping** was tried and rejected (question 3): I preferred keeping the
   written punctuation and sampling observed sentence openings. Boundary starts greatly
   reduce arbitrary mid-sentence openings, but can still start with punctuation: 25 training
   sentences do, and four of the final 500 outputs start with quotes or parentheses.

Final configuration:

```bash
python src/improved_lm.py --start boundary --context 2 --min-len 6 --max-len 30 --novel --seed 376
```

Sample output ([output/improved_final.txt](output/improved_final.txt); none exactly match a cleaned corpus sentence):

> It is time rather to speak .
> These basic precepts are not at fault .
> I will faithfully observe the rights and equal citizenship .
> This Administration has come to us all .
> God bless you and God bless America .
> Universal suffrage should rest upon the whole Government and those reserved to the proper occasion .
> Passion has swept some of its whole constitutional vigor , and uncompromising opponent of every free citizen everywhere to place the cause of peace .

"God bless you and God bless America ." is a nice illustration: the exact sentence never
appears in any address, but the model stitched two formulaic endings together into something
a president could plausibly say. The failures are just as instructive:

> Look , folks , all the markets of the advisers of my countrymen are just and advantageous to both purposes .
> We need all our earthly hopes are entwined .

The first combines Biden's 2021 "Look, folks" with older formal diction, and the second
uses observed trigrams but is ungrammatical as a whole. Two tokens of context provide no
explicit representation of topic, era, or long-range agreement. Further experiments could
try smoothing or interpolation across orders, or a larger corpus (`--corpus state_union`
or `gutenberg`), although those changes alone do not give this model longer-range memory.

These changes produced some plausible short inaugural-sounding sentences, while others
remain fragments or incoherent combinations. The measurements show better sentence openings,
shorter outputs, and fewer exact corpus copies; they do not establish that most outputs
are coherent.
